Key takeaways

  • Five phases: recording, editing, show notes, publishing and promotion, each with its own deadline.
  • Typical deadlines: editing 3-7 days after recording; show notes 1-2 days before the publish date.
  • Work backwards: start from the publish date and set every earlier deadline from it.
  • Build in buffer: if an edit usually takes 3 days, give it 4, so delays don't cascade into missed publish dates.

Every podcast episode follows a timeline: it starts with a recording session, moves through editing, gets wrapped in show notes, and finally publishes to your feed. The trouble is that each step has its own deadline, and they're all interconnected.

  • If recording runs late, editing gets squeezed.
  • If editing takes longer than expected, publishing gets pushed back.
  • If you're not tracking these deadlines explicitly, episodes miss their publish dates regularly.

The real challenge emerges when you're running multiple episodes in parallel. One episode might be in editing while another is being recorded and a third is scheduled to publish tomorrow. Without explicit tracking of each phase for each episode, you lose track of which episode is where. Something falls through, a deadline gets missed, and your listeners notice the episode didn't arrive when they expected it.

The Production Pipeline

Think of your production workflow as a series of phases, each with a start date, an end date, and a deadline. Here's the pattern most podcasters follow:

PhaseWhat happensDeadline to track
RecordingYou and your guest record, in one session or several for long-form conversationsRecording date (or date range if multi-part) and raw length
EditingSilence removed, intros/outros added, levels balanced, issues fixedTypically 3-7 days after recording
Show notesSummary, timestamps, links to resources, guest informationUsually 1-2 days before publish date
PublishingEpisode goes live, is uploaded to platforms and announcedThe publish date: your hard deadline
PromotionClips, emails, social posts, sponsor notificationsFlexible, but worth tracking

Recording

What matters for tracking is the recording date and how long the raw recording is. The raw length tells you how much editing work is coming.

Editing

The 3-7 day range depends on how complex your show is and how much outsourcing you use. Track the editing deadline explicitly so you know when this phase must be complete.

Show notes

You write show notes while editing happens, or after. They often depend on the edited episode, so this phase overlaps with editing.

Publishing and promotion

Everything before the publish date has to be finished, so lock that date in weeks in advance and plan backwards from it. Promotion comes after publishing. It's flexible, but tracking it means you don't forget to promote episodes.

Working Backwards from Publish Date

The key insight is that you need to work backwards from your publish date. If Episode 5 is scheduled to publish on Friday:

  1. Friday: publish. The date your audience expects.
  2. Wednesday: show notes complete.
  3. Tuesday: editing complete.
  4. Monday: recording done.

If your recording runs long, you immediately know editing gets compressed. Once you recognize the squeeze, you can either reschedule the recording or push the publish date.

Without trackingWith tracking
You realize at 4pm Thursday that the episode still isn't edited and it's supposed to publish tomorrow.You see Thursday morning that editing is behind, and you have time to address it.
You panic and rush the edit. Quality suffers, or you miss the publish date and disappoint your audience.You find extra editing time Thursday, or discuss moving the publish date before your audience expects a new episode.

Managing Multiple Episodes

This becomes critical when you're producing multiple episodes per week. If you publish two episodes weekly, you typically have:

  • Two recordings scheduled per week
  • Two concurrent editing projects
  • Two show notes documents in progress
  • Two publish dates at different times

A single tracking system showing where each episode sits in the pipeline prevents mistakes. At a glance, you can see that Episode 7 is being recorded this week, Episode 8 is in editing and due Friday, Episode 9 publishes Monday morning, and Episode 10 needs recording scheduled. Each episode has clear deadlines, and each person on the team (if you have one) knows what they're responsible for and when.

Pro tip: build in buffer time

If your typical edit takes 3 days, set the editing deadline for 4 days after recording. If show notes usually take 1 day, give 1.5 days. This buffer catches delays without cascading into missed publish dates.

Why It Matters

Consistency builds audience loyalty. If your listeners know new episodes arrive every Friday at 9am, that's a promise. Missing that deadline occasionally is forgettable. Missing it regularly damages trust.

Explicit tracking of production timelines helps you keep that promise. You know weeks in advance which episodes publish when, and you manage the workflow to hit those dates reliably.
