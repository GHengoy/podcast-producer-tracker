Key takeaways

  • The rule: pick the slowest cadence you can sustain indefinitely, then hold it.
  • Solo or duo shows: weekly is the realistic ceiling; biweekly or monthly is often the better long-term choice.
  • Consistency beats frequency: a reliable every-other-Tuesday show outperforms one that publishes "whenever it's ready."
  • Use data, not gut feel: time three episodes, check your capacity, then slow the result down one notch.

Every new podcaster asks the same question in the first month: how often should I actually publish? There's no universal number, but there is a reliable way to find the right cadence for your show, your workload, and your audience.

Picking the wrong frequency is one of the most common reasons shows stall out or quietly disappear after a few months.

The Short Answer

Pick the slowest cadence you can sustain indefinitely, then hold it. Most successful independent podcasts settle on weekly or biweekly releases.

Shows that publish daily or multiple times a week are almost always backed by a team, not a solo host recording between other work. If you're producing alone or with one collaborator, weekly is the realistic ceiling, and biweekly or monthly is often the better long-term choice.

Weekly: The Default for a Reason

Weekly is the most common cadence for two reasons:

  • It rewards consistency. It matches how listening apps and algorithms reward a steady release pattern.
  • It gives you just enough runway. A seven-day cycle lets you book a guest, record, edit, and publish without constant panic.

The tradeoff is that weekly leaves almost no slack. One cancelled guest, one sick week, or one busy stretch at your day job and you're publishing late or skipping a week entirely.

Weekly only works if...

Your recording-to-publish pipeline is tight and your guest booking stays two to three weeks ahead of your recording date.

Biweekly: The Sustainable Middle Ground

Biweekly schedules get a bad reputation as "not serious enough." But they're often the most sustainable choice for shows produced by one or two people alongside other work.

A two-week cycle gives you a full week of buffer for guest rescheduling, editing delays, or life getting in the way. You still publish often enough to stay in listeners' rotation. Many shows that start weekly and burn out within three months would have lasted years on a biweekly schedule instead.

Monthly and Seasonal: Fewer, Deeper Episodes

Seasonal means publishing eight to twelve episodes over a few months, then taking a break. Monthly or seasonal releases work well for:

  • Interview shows built around in-depth research
  • Narrative or investigative formats
  • Hosts juggling podcasting around a full-time job or business

The risk is audience memory. Listeners forget to check for new episodes if the gap between releases gets too long without a clear pattern.

Tell listeners the schedule

If you choose a monthly or seasonal cadence, communicate it explicitly in your show notes and social posts so listeners know when to expect the next episode.

Consistency Matters More Than Frequency

A show that publishes reliably every other Tuesday will outperform a show that publishes "whenever it's ready," even if the second show technically releases more episodes per year.

Listeners build a habit around your show. Podcast apps also surface shows with a predictable release pattern more favorably than ones with erratic gaps. If you have to choose between publishing more often and publishing on a schedule you can guarantee, choose the schedule you can guarantee every time.

A Simple Framework for Choosing Your Cadence

  1. Time audit. Track how many hours one episode actually takes you, from guest outreach through final publish, across your next three episodes. Most new podcasters underestimate this by half.
  2. Capacity check. Divide your available weekly production hours by that per-episode number.
  3. Buffer for life. Whatever cadence that math suggests, slow it down by one notch. If the math says weekly, plan for biweekly.

You can always increase frequency later once you have a production rhythm. It's much harder to walk back a cadence you've publicly committed to.

Signs Your Schedule Isn't Sustainable

  • Rushed editing. You're consistently finishing edits the night before publish instead of days ahead.
  • Guest backlog thinning. You no longer have guests confirmed more than one or two episodes out.
  • Skipped weeks increasing. You've missed your stated publish date two or more times in the last quarter.
  • Dread instead of momentum. Recording day feels like a deadline to survive rather than something you look forward to.

Slow down, don't push harder

Any one of these is a sign to slow your cadence before you burn out entirely, not a sign to push harder.
