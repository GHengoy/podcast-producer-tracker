Key takeaways

  • Five stages: every guest moves through outreach, confirmation, preparation, recording and completion.
  • Capture data at each stage: outreach context, confirmation status, prep sent date, recording date and time, and published date.
  • Email doesn't scale: at two episodes a week you're juggling 8-10 active guest conversations, and threads bury the details.
  • One place for everything: a single tracker prevents double-bookings and forgotten follow-ups, and lets you answer guest questions in thirty seconds.

Managing guest appearances on a podcast is a multi-step process that falls apart quickly without a system. From the first outreach email to the moment they're sitting in the recording session, dozens of things need to stay on track. Contact information, confirmation status, prep materials sent, recording date, cancellations, reschedules: if any of these slip through the cracks, your episode doesn't happen.

The typical freelancer approach is to manage guests through email:

  1. Outreach. You send an outreach email, and they respond (eventually).
  2. Back-and-forth. You confirm a date over several messages.
  3. Prep. You send prep materials.
  4. Reminders. A week before recording, you send a reminder. An hour before, you send a calendar invite.

This works for one or two guests a month. But if you're aiming for two episodes a week, you're juggling 8-10 active guest conversations at any given time. Email threads become a nightmare to search. Details get buried. Follow-ups get missed.

The Guest Coordination Pipeline

A proper guest workflow has five clear stages: outreach, confirmation, preparation, recording, and completion. Each stage has specific actions and data that need to be captured.

StageWhat happensWhat to track
OutreachYou identify a guest, find their email, and send an invitation.Name, email, show they're invited to, date you reached out, why you invited them
ConfirmationThey accept or decline. If they decline, you move on to the next prospect.Confirmation status
PreparationYou send prep materials and set the recording date and time.What you sent and when; recording date and time
RecordingThe guest shows up (or logs in if remote) and you record.Completed status, episode length, technical issues, follow-up items
CompletionAfter publishing, you circle back with the guest.Published status; archived guest info

Outreach

Note why you invited each guest: common interests, shared audience, relevant expertise. Without this, when a guest responds weeks later, you won't remember the context.

Confirmation

If they accept, you've got a tentative commitment. This single data point determines whether the episode slot is filled or empty. If confirmation takes a week and you're managing ten outreach conversations, you need one place to see which guests have actually confirmed.

Preparation

Once a guest confirms, send prep materials:

  • Episode topic
  • Format outline
  • Technical requirements
  • Previous episodes they should listen to

Track what you sent and when. If a guest says "I never got the prep materials," you have proof you sent them. The recording date and time you set here becomes your hard deadline for production planning.

Recording and completion

After recording, mark the guest as completed and note episode length, any technical issues, or follow-up items. This becomes part of your production timeline tracking. After publishing, circle back with listener numbers, thank-you notes, or clip promotions the guest can share. Then mark their appearance as published and archive their information in case they want to return.

Why Structure Matters

At each stage, you're storing and accessing specific information. Without structure, you're mentally assembling this data from email threads, Slack messages, and calendar invites. With structure, everything lives in one place, and you can see instantly:

  • Which guests have confirmed, and which are still pending a response
  • Which recording dates are booked
  • Which prep materials were sent, and when
  • Which episodes have been published

Structure also prevents double-bookings and forgotten follow-ups. If you track guest confirmations in a spreadsheet, you can see at a glance whether your Tuesday 2pm slot is free or already booked.

Two simple rules that prevent dropped guests

  • Seven-day follow-up: flag any guest who hasn't confirmed within 7 days of outreach and send a follow-up.
  • Monday prep batch: send prep-material emails on Monday mornings to every guest recording that week.

Most importantly, structure scales the operation. With two guests a month, you remember everything. With ten guests a month across two shows, structure is the only thing standing between smooth coordination and chaos.

A guest asks "what time should I log in?" You pull up their name, find the recording date and time, and send confirmation in thirty seconds. That's the difference between a professional operation and a scrambling amateur.
