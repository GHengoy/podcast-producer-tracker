Episode Tracking FAQ: Your Questions Answered

2026-08-09

← Back to the Complete Podcast Production Guide

As we talk with podcasters about organizing their shows, similar questions come up repeatedly. Here are the most common ones and the answers that work for successful shows.

Do I Really Need to Track All These Details?

Short answer: yes, but you can start small. You don't need to fill in every column from day one. Start with the essentials: episode number, guest name, recording date, publish date, and confirmation status. Once those become routine, add sponsorship details and affiliate links. Add promotion status after publishing is locked down. Build the system in phases as your show grows.

That said, the more detail you track, the more insight you gain. Halfway through the year, you'll have data on which episodes took longest to edit, which guests became regular contributors, which sponsorships converted well. That data is gold for scaling your show. So while you can start small, plan to expand tracking over time.

How Far in Advance Should I Plan Episodes?

Aim to have your next 8-12 weeks locked in the tracker before recording. For each episode, you need: confirmed guest (or topic), recording date, and publish date. This gives you a buffer to handle schedule changes without cascading problems.

In practice, this means: this week you're recording next week's episode, editing last week's, and publishing this week's episode. While doing that, you're outreaching to guests for episodes four weeks out. Your tracker shows all of this simultaneously.

Don't lock in publish dates more than 12 weeks out—life happens and schedules change. But having a rolling 12-week view lets you spot conflicts and capacity problems before they hit.

What If a Guest Cancels Last Minute?

This happens. Your tracker should have a "reschedule" or "cancelled" status so you can mark what happened. If a guest cancels with only a week until publish date, you have two options: find a replacement guest quickly (hard) or publish a solo episode or clip show (easier). Whichever you choose, your tracker shows you that slot needs filling immediately.

Pro tip: Keep a list of backup guests—people you've interviewed before who can jump in on short notice if someone cancels. When you lose a guest, you reach out to your backup list and often can fill the slot within 24-48 hours.

How Do I Handle Podcast Sponsorships If I Don't Have Many?

If you only have sponsorships occasionally, you still want to track them. You might have three episodes with one sponsor in month one, zero sponsored episodes for two months, then five sponsored episodes the next month. Without tracking, you lose the pattern. You might skip adding sponsor ads to an episode and not get paid for them. Or you might accidentally run an ad for Sponsor A on an episode that was supposed to feature Sponsor B.

Even if you have no sponsorships yet, fill in that column with "organic" or "no sponsor" so you're thinking about sponsorship from the beginning. When you do land a sponsor, you'll already have a system to manage them professionally.

Can I Track This in Google Sheets or Does It Need Special Software?

Google Sheets works fine if you design the structure right. A column per data point (date, episode number, guest, status, etc.) and one row per episode is the format that works. Sheets let you sort, filter, and color-code episodes by status. The limitations: Sheets doesn't flag deadlines automatically, doesn't send reminders, and becomes hard to manage once you've got more than 50-60 episodes.

Specialized podcast trackers (including the Podcast Producer & Episode Management Tracker) add features that spreadsheets can't: automatic deadline warnings, filtering by show or status, better data validation, and built-in workflows that guide you through each phase of production. But you can start with Sheets if that's what you have today. The important thing is to start tracking, whether in Sheets, Airtable, or a purpose-built tool.

How Much Time Does Tracking Actually Take?

Adding a new episode to the tracker takes two minutes: put in the guest name, recording date, and publish date. Updating it as you progress (marking guest as confirmed, adding episode title after recording, marking edited) is thirty seconds per update. Over the course of producing an episode, you're spending five-ten minutes total in the tracker across all updates.

That small time investment prevents hours of search-time when you're looking for a guest's email, digging for when an episode was recorded, or trying to remember which sponsorship belonged to which episode. The time savings compound.

What If I'm Behind on Publishing? Does Tracking Help?

Absolutely. If you're behind, the first thing to do is look at your tracker. You immediately see which episodes are in editing (and when they need to finish), which are waiting for show notes, which have publish dates coming up. You can see which episodes can be finished this week and reprioritize accordingly.

Tracking also prevents falling further behind. If you see that three episodes have editing deadlines on Friday and you know you can only finish two, you proactively push one to the following week instead of rushing it and creating a quality problem.

Should I Track Listener Numbers?

If you have access to listener metrics (downloads, listening time, completion rate), tracking those by episode is incredibly valuable. Over time, you see which episodes get more listens and which topics resonated. You can correlate promotion efforts with listening spikes. You can see if live guests drive more listeners than pre-recorded content.

You don't need this data on day one. But once your show has a few months of episodes and you're getting consistent listener data, add a "listeners" column to your tracker. Six months of data will reveal patterns that guide your content strategy.

What's the Minimum Viable Tracker?

If you're just starting and don't want to overthink it, track these six columns: Date (publish date), Episode Number, Guest Name, Recording Date, Publish Date, and Status (pending/recorded/edited/published). That's enough to keep your pipeline organized. Add other columns as you need them.

How Do I Know If My Tracking System Is Working?

Three signs your tracker is actually working: (1) You never miss a publish date because you're referencing deadlines ahead of time. (2) You can answer "what's the status of episode X?" in ten seconds by looking at your tracker. (3) You're not searching email for guest details or recording dates anymore—it's all in the tracker.

If you're still searching email, missing deadlines, or feeling confused about which episode is where, your tracking system needs improvement. Either it's not detailed enough, or you're not checking it often enough. Go back to the system and add the details or time blocks that will fix the gap.
